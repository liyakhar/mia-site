Where to give birth: home, birth centre or hospital

You have a choice about where to give birth. Each place is explained fairly, so you can decide with your midwife.

Summary
  • At homeWith a midwife present. Usually only advised for a straightforward pregnancy. You can move to hospital at any time.
  • Midwife-led unitA homelike setting with midwives. Medical help is nearby or on site.
  • HospitalBest if you or baby need extra care. Doctors and pain relief such as epidural are available.
  • Ask your midwifeYour history matters. Ask what is safest and what is available near you.
01

Your choices

You can give birth at home, in a midwife-led unit or birth centre, or in hospital. Where you give birth is up to you, and you can change your mind at any point in pregnancy.12

If your pregnancy is high risk or you have certain conditions, it is safest to give birth in hospital, where specialists are available.1

You are free to choose any maternity service if you are willing to travel. Ask your midwife about visiting local units.1

02

Home birth

It is usually recommended only if you have a straightforward pregnancy and you and your baby are well. A midwife stays with you during labour and arranges a move to hospital if needed.1

If it is your first baby, a home birth slightly increases the risk of serious problems for the baby, from 5 in 1,000 for a hospital birth to 9 in 1,000. If you have had a baby before, a planned home birth is as safe as hospital or a midwife-led unit.12

The benefits include familiar surroundings, not having to interrupt labour to travel, and, if your pregnancy is low risk, a lower chance of forceps or ventouse.1

You cannot have an epidural at home, but you can have gas and air, a warm bath or pool, TENS and relaxation. Some NHS trusts do not offer pethidine at home.12

03

Midwife-led unit or birth centre

These are more homely than a hospital ward. Some are part of a hospital and some are separate, with no immediate obstetric or anaesthetic care.1

If low risk, you are less likely to need forceps or ventouse. In a separate unit you cannot have an epidural, so ask whether yours is attached to a hospital.1

04

Hospital

Most births happen in an NHS hospital maternity unit. You get direct access to obstetricians, anaesthetists for epidurals, and newborn specialists.1

You are more likely to have an epidural, an episiotomy or a forceps or ventouse delivery in hospital. You may also see a different midwife from the one in pregnancy.1

05

Transferring to hospital

You can change your mind and go to hospital at any time, even in labour.2

You will need to move if labour is not progressing or if there are complications, for example bleeding, a baby who has passed meconium, or waters that broke 18 or more hours before labour.2

Ask your midwife how long a transfer would take, which hospital you would go to, and whether a midwife will be with you the whole time.1

06

If you have a risk factor

You may be advised to give birth in hospital if you have pre-eclampsia or high blood pressure, diabetes, a high BMI, abnormal scans, a previous caesarean, a breech baby, a multiple pregnancy, or a previous premature birth.2

Your midwife or doctor will explain why they think hospital is safer.12

Good to know
Is a home birth safe?

Giving birth is generally safe wherever you choose. For a first baby, home birth slightly raises the risk of serious problems for the baby. For second and later babies, a planned home birth is as safe as hospital.

Can I change my mind in labour?

Yes. You can go to hospital at any time, and your midwife can organise it.
