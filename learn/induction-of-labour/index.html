Induction of labour: why, how and what to ask

Induction means starting labour with medical help. This guide covers why it is offered, how it works and the questions worth asking.

Summary
  • Why it is offeredCommon reasons are going past your due date, your waters breaking without labour, or a health concern for you or baby.
  • How it worksYou are first offered a membrane sweep. Induction itself uses hormones or a small balloon, and happens in hospital.
  • It can take timeInduction often takes longer than labour that starts on its own. Bring things to pass the time.
  • Ask questionsAsk what the risks are of both inducing and waiting, and what your other choices are.
01

What induction is

Induction starts labour artificially. It is common if your baby is overdue or there is a risk to you or your baby’s health.1

It is usually planned in advance, and it is your choice whether to have it.1

02

Why you might be offered it

If you have not gone into labour by 41 weeks, induction is offered because the risk of stillbirth or problems for the baby is higher.1

If your pregnancy lasts longer than 42 weeks and you decide not to be induced, you should be offered increased monitoring.1

If your waters break more than 24 hours before labour starts, there is a higher risk of infection. After 34 weeks you can choose induction or expectant management, where you are monitored and the pregnancy progresses naturally while it is safe.1

You may also be offered it for a health condition such as diabetes, high blood pressure or intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, or if your baby is not growing well.1

03

A membrane sweep first

Before induction you will be offered a membrane sweep, usually after 39 weeks. Your midwife or doctor sweeps a finger around your cervix to separate the membranes, which releases hormones that may start labour.1

It can be uncomfortable or painful, with some cramping and bleeding afterwards.1

04

How it is done

Induction is always done in a hospital maternity unit. There are 2 main ways: hormones as a vaginal tablet or gel, or a device such as a small water balloon or a sponge to widen your cervix. You will usually be offered hormones first.1

It usually takes many hours to start working. Sometimes a hormone drip or breaking your waters is needed to speed things up.1

05

What to expect

Induced labour is usually more painful than labour that starts alone, and you are more likely to have an assisted delivery with forceps or ventouse.1

Your pain relief options are not restricted by being induced. You can still have an epidural or a water birth.1

Induction does not always work. You may be offered another method or a caesarean, or you may wait a few hours and be assessed again.1

06

Natural ways to start labour

There are no proven ways to start labour yourself at home. There is no evidence that hot baths, certain foods or drinks, sex or herbal supplements work.1

There is no safety information on herbal supplements, and they could harm you or your baby. Sex will not cause harm, but avoid it if your waters have broken.1

Call
Call your midwife if
  • Your waters break
  • Your baby moves less than usual
  • Vaginal bleeding
  • You have not gone into labour by 41 weeks. Ask about your options
Good to know
Do I have to be induced?

No. It is your choice. If you decide against it after 42 weeks, you should be offered increased monitoring of your baby.

Does a membrane sweep hurt?

Some people find it uncomfortable or painful, and you may have cramping or light bleeding afterwards.

Can I bring on labour myself?

There are no proven ways to safely do this at home. Ask your midwife before trying anything.
