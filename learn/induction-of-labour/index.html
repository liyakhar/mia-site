Induction of labour: what to expect

Induction means starting labour with medical help. Here is why it is offered, how it works and the questions worth asking.

Why induction is offered

Induction means starting labour artificially. It is offered when waiting longer may be riskier for you or your baby than starting labour now.

Common reasons are going past 41 weeks, your waters breaking without labour starting, or health concerns like high blood pressure or diabetes.

How it works

It often starts with a membrane sweep. Then a pessary or gel to soften the cervix, or a small balloon.

Next, your waters may be broken, and a hormone drip (oxytocin) may be used to start contractions.

How long it takes

Induction can take a day or more, especially with a first baby. Bring things to pass the time and snacks.

What to ask

Ask: why is it advised for me? What are the risks of waiting? What are the options, and can I go home during the early part?

When to call your midwife

  • Waters break, bleeding, or your baby moves less while you wait

Maternity units are open day and night. Calling is never a waste of anyone’s time.

Based on NICE guideline on inducing labour and NHS guidance. Mia teaches. Your midwife knows you best.

Sources: NICE guideline NG207: Inducing labour · NHS: Inducing labour

Good to know

Can I say no to induction?

Yes. It is your decision. Your team will explain the risks and benefits so you can choose.

Does a membrane sweep hurt?

It can feel uncomfortable or crampy for a short time.

Is induction more painful?

Contractions can feel stronger, and more people ask for an epidural.
