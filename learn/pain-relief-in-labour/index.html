Pain relief in labour: every option compared

There is no right or wrong way to cope with labour. Every option is covered fairly, so you can choose and change your mind.

Summary

  • Without medicine. Warm water, massage, movement, breathing and a calm room can all help.
  • TENS and gas and air. Gas and air takes 15 to 20 seconds to work and you control it yourself. TENS is most useful in early labour.
  • Injections. Pethidine or similar can take the edge off pain but may make you or baby drowsy.
  • Epidural. In most cases an epidural gives complete pain relief. It can lengthen the pushing stage, so forceps or ventouse may be needed.

Help yourself first

You are likely to cope better if you learn about labour, learn to relax and breathe deeply, and keep moving. Try kneeling, walking or rocking.NHS

A support person, a massage, a warm bath and paracetamol in early labour can all help. Keep an open mind, because you may want more pain relief than you planned.NHS

Gas and air

Gas and air is oxygen and nitrous oxide. It does not remove all the pain but makes it more bearable, and you control it yourself. It takes 15 to 20 seconds to work, so breathe it in as a contraction begins.NHS

It can make you light-headed, sick or sleepy. If it does, you can stop. Using it for more than 24 hours can lower your vitamin B12.NHS

Injections

Pethidine or, less often, diamorphine, is injected into your thigh or buttock. It takes about 20 minutes to work and lasts 2 to 4 hours, so it is not recommended close to the pushing stage.NHS

It can make you woozy and sick. Given too close to the birth it can affect your baby’s breathing, and it can interfere with the first feed.NHS

Epidural

An epidural numbs the nerves that carry pain from the birth canal. In most cases it gives complete pain relief. Only an anaesthetist can give one, so it is not available at home.NHS

It takes about 10 minutes to set up and 10 to 15 more to work. Your contractions and baby’s heart rate are monitored continuously.NHS

Side effects can include heavy legs, difficulty peeing, and a longer pushing stage, which can mean forceps or ventouse are needed. A headache happens in about 1 in 100 cases. Epidurals do not cause long-term backache.NHS

Remifentanil, water and TENS

Remifentanil goes into a vein and you control it with a button. It works quickly and wears off in minutes. It can make you sleepy, sick or itchy, and may briefly affect your baby’s breathing.NHS

Water can help you relax and make contractions seem less painful. The water is kept at a comfortable temperature, not above 37.5°C.NHS

TENS has not been shown to work in active labour. It is probably most helpful in early labour, especially for lower back pain, and has no known side effects.NHS

Other methods

Acupuncture, aromatherapy, homeopathy, hypnosis, massage and reflexology are options, but most are not proven to give effective pain relief. Most hospitals do not offer them.NHS

If you want to try them, talk to your midwife first and choose a properly trained practitioner.NHS

Good to know

Can I change my mind?

Yes. Write your wishes in your birth plan but keep an open mind. You can ask for more pain relief at any point.

Will an epidural slow my labour?

It can lengthen the pushing stage, so forceps or ventouse may be needed. Your midwife may wait longer before you push to lower that chance.
